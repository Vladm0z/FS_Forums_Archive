Have we been looking at weapon and blessing balancing all wrong?

19 Posts • 353 Views
A
AoyeLaozei • 2026-06-20 21:46:02

First and foremost, let’s look at the core loop of Darktide. Whether we are tackling regular Auric missions, high-intensity Maelstroms, or Havoc mode, our objective is always singular, and our path is strictly linear. Sure, you might occasionally detour through a side room to avoid a Daemonhost, but the reality is that we are constantly moving through claustrophobic, enclosed corridors. Even in the handful of missions that offer slightly larger environments, those spaces are so cluttered with cover and debris that our actual lanes of fire and tactical positioning remain heavily restricted.

As a result, we are perpetually forced into a single playstyle: grinding down endless, overwhelming hordes of enemies in tight spaces. While this intense, unforgiving gameplay is undoubtedly the soul of Darktide, it drastically suffocates weapon variety. For players who joined this game out of a deep love for the Warhammer 40,000 IP, this is incredibly disappointing. This frustration is highly visible right here on the Fatshark forums. Players are tired of the stagnant, repetitive meta of choosing the exact same weapons and blessing combinations. Consequently, the community often defaults to demanding nerfs for “overpowered” or “cheese” builds, hoping it will somehow carve out a viable niche for underutilized gear.

But I want us to take a step back and ask ourselves: is a never-ending cycle of nerfs truly the only way forward? Today, we demand nerfs for the Plasma Gun and the Veteran’s Power Sword because they feel “overpowered.” But what happens tomorrow? When everyone inevitably shifts to the Revolver and the Lucius Lasgun, do we just demand nerfs for those next?

In my opinion, the root of the problem isn’t the weapons themselves; it’s the rigid team compositions and mandatory class responsibilities forced upon us in high-pressure environments. In a typical high-difficulty run, an ideal squad is strictly locked into four distinct niches:

Specialist/Elite Hunter (Anti-Special): To instantly snipe Gunners, Bombers, and Snipers.

Frontline/Tank: To hold the line against crushing waves of Ogryns and Carapace armor, keeping the threat away from squishier teammates.

Horde Clear: To rapidly blend trash mobs and create breathing room for the team.

Boss Killer: To burning down Bosses/Monsters before they wipe the squad.

On top of these rigid roles, a team must also pack massive utility and room for error—which is why you almost always see mandatory picks like the Veteran’s VoC, Smoke Grenades, or the Psyker’s Telekine Shield. The moment a squad lacks even one of these pillars, the success rate plummets, the mechanical demand on players skyrockets, and the pressure becomes greater.

In high-difficulty, claustrophobic environments, abilities like the Ogryn’s Bull Rush, the Veteran’s or Zealot’s Infiltrate/Chastise, or a Psyker choosing not to bring the Telekine Shield can almost feel “selfish.” They often end up trading overall team survivability just to boost the individual’s own odds of staying alive.

When the “Beyond the Hive” mode was first introduced, the community had massive expectations. While the execution fell short for many—as we essentially just ran around larger spaces doing minigames—it did offer a massive silver lining. The vast, open-ended layout of Beyond the Hive gave us multiple firing lines and non-linear paths. Suddenly, team composition didn’t feel entirely set in stone.

The open terrain allowed players to adopt entirely new combat roles that are non-existent in the standard game:

Assault: Armed with Infantry Autoguns (which are rarely viable in normal Damnation+), players could sprint across open ground, intercept an incoming patrol of Ragers, and melt most of them before they even closed the distance.

Sniper: Taking up secure high-ground positions to overlook the battlefield, picking off high-threat targets while teammates engaged the horde below.

Fire Support: Laying down heavy, sustained suppressive fire to lock down enemy Gunners, giving the rest of the squad the breathing room to clear out the open fields.

In this environment, squad flexibility flourished. Choices that seemed entirely “selfish” in narrow corridors suddenly came to life and found a genuine, tactical purpose in maps that offered space, options, and freedom.

Perhaps, instead of constantly demanding Fatshark to nerf blessings and weapons, our collective voice would be much better spent pushing them to design better gameplay loops and mechanics.

We should be advocating for meaningful additions to modes like Beyond the Hive—such as introducing dedicated high-value target hunting (e.g., hunting down a specific boss, so the ammunition we expend actually serves a tactical objective, rather than just being wasted on pure self-defense). We need maps with non-linear paths and flexible layouts that allow our squad to operate like a true, elite strike team infiltrating an objective, rather than forcing every single engagement in a mission to feel like a desperate, reactive struggle for survival.

I’d say there is something of a good point here – given the nature of Darktide it is difficult to see how some things would be a good fit normally. OTOH, the ‘tide formula is popular (within its niche, at least) for a reason – people enjoy the hordebashing + elite disrupters gameplay and it seems that deviations from this don’t tend to go well - and I’m somewhat skeptical you could marry things like sniping with the main Darktide game.

From my POV, I’d say that some of the more fundamental issues are (1) lack of granularity in difficulty modes; (2) lack of onboarding for inexperienced players; (3) the plethora of mechanics, weapons, and class builds (combined with the dramatic variability of non-controlled factors, such as enemy teleportation, teammate competence, etc.) which would be a logistical nightmare for anyone.

From a purely personal perspective (I make no grandiose claims about How Darktide Should Be) I would like to see a starting point of: (1) difficulty modes which acclimatise you better with less difficulty spikes and smoother use of enemy density / frequency / danger scaling; (2) better tooltips for what things mean, playable tutorialising and practice modes (e.g. where you can repeatedly practice shoving, dodging, cleaving, sliding, etc. vs active enemies outside of normal gameplay, as well as testing your build against enemies in and out of combat); (3) a redo of the talent trees to make each class have a choice of several different builds with clearly defined roles, and a decrease in overlapping weapons and mechanics (to reduce changes having unintentional downstream effects). Again, just my personal view .
